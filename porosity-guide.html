Guide · Die casting

Porosity is a process signature,
not a supplier's mistake.

Every high-pressure die casting contains gas or shrinkage voids somewhere. The only question that matters on a purchase order is which ones you will accept, where, and how the foundry proves it.

Why the metal cannot fill the die cleanly

In high-pressure die casting the cavity fills in milliseconds. Gate velocities typically run 30–60 m/s, and the air that was in the cavity has to leave through the vents in that same window. Some of it does not. The rest is compressed into the metal and stays there as gas porosity — round, smooth-walled voids, usually 0.2–2 mm, sitting near the surface or at the end of the flow path.

The second family is shrinkage. A section that is thicker than its neighbours solidifies last, and the volume it loses on solidification has nothing to draw from. That produces jagged, dendritic cavities, typically at bosses, rib junctions and heavy flanges. The two types behave differently downstream: gas porosity expands and blisters when the part is painted or powder-coated, shrinkage porosity opens up when a sealing face is machined.

That distinction is the reason a single line in a drawing — "no porosity allowed" — is unusable. It asks the foundry to reject parts that no die casting process can produce, and the practical result is either a price nobody can hold or a supplier who quietly ignores the note.

Typical wall thickness for aluminium HPDC sits around 2.0–4.0 mm. Below roughly 1.5 mm the flow length to thickness ratio becomes the limiting factor; above about 6 mm shrinkage porosity becomes hard to avoid without local cooling or squeeze pins. Both numbers are process rules of thumb, not standard limits — the die layout decides.

Three ways to accept or reject, and what each one really measures

Visual. Only finds surface-breaking defects. Appropriate for non-critical cosmetic faces, and inexpensive, but it says nothing about the inside of the part. If your drawing says "visual inspection only", do not later reject a batch because a machined face shows a pore — the criterion never covered it.

Radiography. ASTM E505 provides reference radiographs for aluminium and magnesium die castings with severity levels from 1 to 8, level 1 being the least severe. A drawing note should read something like "ASTM E505, gas porosity not exceeding severity level 3 in the sealing zone, level 5 elsewhere". That is inspectable, comparable between foundries, and it prices. The trap is specifying level 2 across a whole part: at that point you are asking for a casting process that is closer to gravity casting in cost, and the quote will reflect it.

Pressure or leak test. The only method that tests the function rather than the appearance. For valve and pump bodies, API 598 is the usual reference: shell test at 1.5 times the rated pressure and seat test at 1.1 times, held for the duration the standard specifies. For non-pressure parts, a decay test at 1–2 bar air with a 30–60 s stabilisation is a common in-house substitute, but it is a customer-specific criterion — it has to be written into the PO, not assumed.

The severity levels and test multipliers above are the ones commonly written into die casting and valve purchase orders. They are typical practice, not a substitute for the standard text or for your own end-customer's requirement. Confirm the exact clause with your quality department before it goes on a drawing.

Where the money goes: radiographing every part on a 20,000-piece order can add more to the unit price than the casting itself. First article plus a sampling frequency — for example five parts per shift, or per 2,000 pieces — gives you the same process control at a fraction of the cost. Write the frequency down, not just the level.

What to put in the RFQ so the quote is comparable

  • Which zones are functional. One sentence and a marked-up drawing. A foundry that does not know which face seals will quote a uniform porosity level and charge you for it.
  • Post-casting operations. If the part is machined, powder-coated or anodised, say so. Gas porosity that is harmless as-cast becomes a reject after a 200 °C cure or a caustic etch.
  • Test pressure and medium. Air, water, or helium changes both the sensitivity and the equipment the supplier needs.
  • Who pays for the X-ray film and the report. Documents are a line item in some quotes and included in others; the difference shows up when you compare prices.
  • What happens on a failed lot. Rework, concession or scrap, and who decides. Agreeing this before the first shipment is cheaper than arguing about it during one.

The question behind the question

Buyers search for "acceptable porosity in die casting" because they have received parts with visible pores and do not know whether to reject them. The useful answer is not a single number. It is a zoning decision: pressure-tight where it seals, structural where it carries load, cosmetic where it is seen, and unconstrained everywhere else. Once that is on the drawing, porosity stops being an argument and becomes a measurement.
