Sourcing guide · Injection molding

Two quotes, same part,
3.1× apart. Here is why.

A buyer sends the same STEP file to six Chinese mold shops and gets quotes from ¥28,000 to ¥86,000 for a single-cavity tool. Almost none of that spread is profit margin. It is four decisions made differently — steel grade, cavity count, runner type, and how many trial shots are included.

1. The six lines in every mold quote

Ask for the quote broken down this way. A shop that will not break it down is either guessing or hiding something.

LineWhat it coversShare of tool cost
Mold baseStandard plate set, ejector system, guide pillars10 – 18%
Cavity & core steelInserts, rough and finish machining, heat treatment30 – 40%
Slides, lifters, unscrewingAny motion beyond open-and-close0 – 20%
Runner systemCold runner (cheap) or hot runner (expensive)2 – 25%
Design & programming3D mold design, DFM, CAM, electrode design8 – 14%
Trial shots & correctionT1 sample, dimensional report, one round of rework5 – 12%

Typical split for a single-cavity tool for a 100–300 g ABS or PP part. Small parts with tight tolerances shift weight toward cavity steel; large parts shift toward the base and handling.

2. The four decisions that actually move the number

Cavity steel: P20 versus 718H versus S136

For a part run under 300,000 shots in PP, PE or ABS, pre-hardened P20 is correct and cheap. 718H buys you better polishability and a bit more toughness; it matters for visible surfaces and for glass-filled material. S136 or similar stainless is for PVC, medical parts, and anything that will sit wet — not because it lasts longer, but because P20 rusts and a rusted cavity produces scrap.

The mistake is buying steel for a shot count nobody has committed to. A 500,000-shot mold on a product that sells 20,000 a year is money parked in a block of steel.

Cavity count: 1×1, 2×2, 4×4

Adding cavities does not double the tool cost — the base and the design work are shared. Going from one cavity to two typically adds 55–75% to tool cost and roughly halves the per-part cycle cost; four cavities add about 1.4–1.8× the single-cavity price. The crossover is arithmetic: multiply annual volume by the per-part saving and compare it with the extra tool cost.

Hot runner

A hot runner system adds ¥12,000–45,000 depending on gate count and brand, and it is the line item buyers most often cut to hit a budget. It earns that back in three ways: no sprue to regrind, faster cycles because the sprue no longer has to cool, and better gate cosmetics. For a 4-cavity tool running engineering resin, it usually pays for itself inside the first year. For a 1×1 tool making 5,000 parts a year, it does not.

How many trial rounds are included

"T1 samples included" means one round. If the part needs steel-safe changes, the second round is a change order. Ask explicitly: how many trial rounds, is a dimensional report included, and who pays for the second one. This single question separates a ¥35,000 quote from a ¥35,000 quote that ends up costing ¥48,000.

3. What looks expensive but is not

  • Mold flow analysis. ¥2,000–6,000, and it finds the weld line or the sink mark before steel is cut. The alternative is finding it at T1 and paying for a re-cut.
  • A proper DFM report. Usually free from a competent shop. If it is not offered, that is the warning sign, not the price.
  • Hard chrome or nitriding on the cavity. ¥800–2,500. On abrasive or corrosive resin it extends life appreciably; on PP it is decoration.

5. Before you send the RFQ

  1. Freeze the 3D model. Quotes based on a model that is still moving are fiction.
  2. State annual volume and expected product life in years, not "high volume".
  3. Name the resin and the grade. "ABS" and "ABS + 20% GF" are different tools.
  4. Say which surfaces are cosmetic. Polish specification drives hours.
  5. Ask for the quote in the six lines above, plus the number of trial rounds.
