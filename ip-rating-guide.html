IP65 is a test result,
not a description of your cabinet.

Buyers search for IP65 and IP66 expecting a material or a design. What the code actually records is a nozzle, a flow rate and a duration that a sample survived once. Everything else — the gasket, the gland, the cutout, the paint — decides whether the delivered unit still behaves that way.

Two digits, two completely different tests

IEC 60529 writes the code as IP followed by two characters. The first is about solid objects and dust, the second about water. They are tested separately and they fail separately, which is why a cabinet can be genuinely dust-tight and still leak, or hose-proof and still fill with fine dust through an unsealed gland plate.

Flow rates, pressures and durations above are the commonly used parameters in IEC 60529 test setups. They are typical values for orientation, not a substitute for the standard text or for the test report your supplier should be able to send you.

The gap between IP65 and IP66 is therefore not a marketing step up. It is a factor of eight in water volume — 12.5 litres per minute against 100 — from a nozzle twice the diameter, at more than three times the pressure. An enclosure that passes IPX5 by a comfortable margin can fail IPX6 on the same gasket, because the failure mode changes from "water runs down the door" to "water is driven into the seal line".

Why NEMA 4 and IP66 are not the same number

NEMA 250 and IEC 60529 were written by different bodies for different purposes. NEMA includes corrosion resistance, gasket ageing and, for some types, an indoor/outdoor distinction. IP does not test corrosion at all. The comparison below is the one buyers usually need, and the right-hand column is where most purchase orders go wrong.

For a control panel assembled to UL 508A, the enclosure type and the panel rating interact: a Type 4 enclosure with unrated field wiring brought in through a standard knockout is no longer a Type 4 panel. That is the point most often missed when a buyer compares a Chinese quotation against a European one — the cheaper unit is frequently rated as an empty box, not as the assembly being shipped.

Where the rating is lost after it leaves the factory

A type test is run on one sample, in a laboratory, with the enclosure closed. Five things change between that sample and the unit on site.

  • Gasket compression. A poured or foamed gasket needs roughly 25–40% compression to seal. A door that is slightly twisted, or hinges fitted without a jig, will meet that figure at three corners and miss it at the fourth. Ask for a flatness check on the door, not just a gasket datasheet.
  • Cable entries. The gland is part of the enclosure. An IP66 box with an IP54 gland and a plain hole is an IP54 assembly. If the buyer supplies their own glands, say so on the drawing and state which rating the cutouts must preserve.
  • Cutouts and windows. Every laser-cut opening, inspection window and display bezel is a new seal line. A window bonded with a gasket that is compressed by screws at 150 mm pitch will bow between fixings.
  • Powder coating on sealing faces. A 60–80 μm film on the flange where the gasket sits is a soft, compressible layer that relaxes over time. Masking the seal face, or specifying a thinner film there, is a cheap way to keep the compression predictable.
  • Condensation. A sealed enclosure with a temperature swing will collect water inside even with no leak at all. That is a pressure-equalisation problem, not a gasket problem, and the usual answer is a breathable vent that passes air and vapour but not liquid water.
Where the money goes: moving a standard wall-mounted box from IP55 to IP66 usually costs a gasket change and a retest. Moving it to IP69K changes the door design, the hinge, the seal material and the surface finish, and the price step is much larger than the code suggests. If the washdown is only occasional, an IP66 box under a drip shield is often the cheaper engineering answer — and it is a decision worth writing down rather than leaving to the supplier.

What to put in the RFQ

  • The rating, the standard and the assembly level. "IP66 to IEC 60529, tested as a complete assembly including glands" is a specification. "IP66" alone is a wish.
  • Who tests and who pays. Type test at a third-party laboratory, or an in-house report? The difference is visible in the price and in the delivery date.
  • The corrosion requirement, separately. Salt spray hours and the standard used, because IP does not measure corrosion and NEMA 4X does not fix the hours.
  • Gland and cutout responsibility. Who supplies the glands, at what rating, and who is accountable if the assembled unit fails.
  • What happens on a failed batch. Re-gasket, re-test or replace, and who pays the freight. Agreeing this before the first container is cheaper than negotiating it after one.

The question behind the question

Buyers search for the difference between IP65 and IP66 because they have been quoted both for the same project and cannot see what changed. The useful answer is not a definition. It is a list: the nozzle and the flow rate, the gland rating, the seal face finish, the test report, and whether the rating applies to the empty box or to the assembly. Once those five are on the drawing, the two quotations become comparable — and the conversation stops being about the code and starts being about the installation.
