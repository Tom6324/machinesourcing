A dash number is a size code,
not a part number.

"−010" tells you the cross-section and the inside diameter in one short string. It says nothing about the material, the hardness, the tolerance class or who made it. Most wrong orders start when those five facts get compressed into the dash number alone.

What the three digits are doing

Under SAE AS568 an O-ring is written as a dash followed by three digits. The first digit selects the cross-section series, which is a fixed nominal cord thickness. The last two digits pick a position along that series' inside-diameter ladder. That is the whole code. It is a lookup key, not a description.

Cross-sections above are the nominal values used across the AS568 series. ISO 3601 uses the same metric figures and does not use dash numbers at all — its sizes are written as inside diameter × cross-section in millimetres.

The 1/16 inch series is the workhorse of small hydraulic and pneumatic fittings, sensor housings and filter bowls. Inside diameters step by 1/16 inch from size to size, which is why the numbers look irregular in metric.

The metric substitution that quietly fails

A 1.78 mm metric ring and a −0xx imperial ring are dimensionally the same cord, and that is exactly where the trap sits. The two systems publish different tolerance bands: AS568 splits sizes into commercial and precision classes, ISO 3601 defines its own classes, and the acceptable inside-diameter spread on a small ring can differ by a few tenths of a millimetre between them.

On a static face seal with plenty of squeeze, a few tenths do not matter. On a dynamic rod seal, or a gland designed to a fixed fill percentage, they do — the ring either drags or leaks, and the buyer blames the material when the problem was the tolerance class. If a drawing calls for a −014, supply a −014 and say which class. If the customer wants metric, quote the metric size with its ISO class and let them approve the change.

Do not measure a used ring to reorder. A ring that has been in service for a year is permanently deformed, swollen by the fluid and often worn flat on the sealing face. Its inside diameter reads large and its cord reads thin. Measure the groove instead — groove width, groove depth and the shaft or bore diameter — and derive the size from the gland. The used ring is evidence of what failed, not a specification.

Reading a size off a groove, in order

  • Measure the groove depth and width. Depth sets the squeeze; width sets the fill. Both are machined dimensions and do not drift with service.
  • Measure the bore or shaft. Inside diameter of the ring equals bore diameter on a piston seal, or shaft diameter on a rod seal, before stretch is applied.
  • Pick the series from the depth. A groove about 1.4 mm deep belongs to a 1.78 mm cord; about 2.2 mm belongs to a 2.62 mm cord. Nominal squeeze on a static face seal usually lands in the 15–25 per cent range.
  • Match the inside diameter to the ladder. Choose the dash whose nominal ID sits closest to the gland diameter, then confirm the resulting stretch is acceptable — 1 to 5 per cent is the usual working band for most static applications.
  • Write the full callout. Dash number, material, hardness and standard, for example: −014, NBR 70 Shore A, AS568 precision class.

What the dash number does not tell you

Four things get left out of almost every RFQ that starts from a dash number, and each one changes the price or the failure mode. The compound decides temperature and fluid compatibility. The hardness decides how the ring behaves under pressure and whether it extrudes. The tolerance class decides how much the delivered ring may vary. The standard — AS568, ISO 3601, or a customer drawing — decides which of the above applies.

When a buyer sends only "−012, 500 pcs", the useful reply is not a price. It is the four questions, asked once, followed by a price that will not need to be revised after the sample arrives.
