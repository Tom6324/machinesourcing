A dash number is not
a part number. It is two numbers.

Every day, maintenance engineers and buyers type "AS568 -214" or "dash number chart" into a search box, and most results either dump a 475-row table or explain nothing. This page is the missing middle: what a dash number actually encodes, how the series are built, and what to check on a quotation before you commit a groove to it.

What the dash number encodes

AS568 (the American standard for aerospace-size O-rings) lists a fixed set of inside-diameter and cross-section combinations. Each row got a sequential number, written with a dash: -004, -110, -214, -334. The dash number itself carries no arithmetic meaning — it is a row index. But the series the number falls into tells you the cross-section, and that is what experienced buyers actually read from it:

SeriesNominal cross-section (CS)Typical use
-001 to -0290.042 in and 0.070 inSmall hydraulics, fittings, instrumentation
-100 to -1780.103 inGeneral hydraulics and pneumatics
-200 to -2840.139 inThe workhorse series — most industrial glands are designed here
-300 to -3960.210 inLarge static flanges, low-pressure vessels
-400 to -4750.275 inBig static seals, tank and manifold covers

Series boundaries and cross-sections are the nominal AS568 values; always confirm the exact row in a current size table (supplier catalogues such as Parker's O-ring handbook reproduce them with tolerances).

Why -214 is the size everyone quotes

Within the -200 series, -214 (nominal ID 1.078 in, CS 0.139 in) has become the default size for general industrial glands — many test rigs, standard cylinders and pump housings are designed around it, which is why it is also the size every sample pack leads with. A supplier who can hold tolerance on -214 in NBR 70 can usually hold the rest of the series; a supplier whose -214 datum sheet looks vague is telling you something about the rest of the table.

What a groove actually does to the ring

The dash number defines the ring, but the gland defines the seal. Two figures on any supplier's groove page decide whether an O-ring survives the year:

  • Squeeze. The gland compresses the ring so it deforms rather than leaks. Typical design practice: roughly 10–20% squeeze on dynamic (reciprocating or rotary) duties and 15–30% on static duties, varying with cross-section and hardness. Too little weeps; too much kills the ring by heat and set.
  • Gland fill. The ring's cross-sectional area must leave room for thermal expansion and squeeze volume — common practice keeps fill at or below about 85%. Overfilled glands are the quiet cause of seals that "fail for no reason" on Monday mornings after a hot weekend.

Squeeze and fill ranges are typical industry practice; final gland dimensions should come from the seal supplier's groove design tables (Parker ORK, Trelleborg and similar handbooks) matched to the compound and duty.

Crossing to metric: ISO 3601

European drawings often specify ISO 3601 class A or B rings instead of AS568 dash numbers. The two systems overlap heavily but are not identical — a nominal "metric equivalent" of -214 exists within ISO 3601's table, but tolerances and class rules differ. When a customer mixes references on one drawing (AS568 dash on the BOM, ISO 3601 class in the note), quote against one system and state the other as reference — this single habit prevents a large share of "wrong size" claims, because the discrepancy is caught at quotation rather than at assembly.

What to check before ordering, in three lines

  1. Compound, not just size. "-214" without a hardness and compound code (NBR 70, FKM 75, EPDM 70) is half a part number. Insist the quote prints all three.
  2. Cure date and shelf life. Elastomers age in storage. NBR stored cool and dark is commonly given a shelf life in the multi-year range per industry guidance, but the bag should carry a cure date so FIFO rotation is possible. No cure date printed = no traceability.
  3. The groove, not the ring. If you have inherited a machine and no drawing, measure the gland — rod or bore diameter, groove width, groove depth — and let the supplier work back to the dash number. Sizing from the old, partly-set ring you dug out of the groove is how the wrong size gets specified forever.

A note for distributors and MRO buyers

If you buy standard dash sizes for stock, the quality difference between suppliers shows up in exactly the paperwork, not the rubber: cure dates on bags, lot traceability, hardness certificates, and willingness to quote from your groove measurements. That paperwork is what separates a factory-direct supply from a trader repacking someone else's bulk bags — and it is checkable on the first sample order, before any volume commitment.
